\section{Data provenance and the 13 October 2026 correction}
\label{sec:data-correction}

\textbf{Every result in this chapter has been regenerated. The figures in
earlier versions did not come from the NASA archive.}

\texttt{data/fetch\_osdr\_data.py} previously returned values written as literals
in its own source --- first generated by \texttt{numpy.random.normal()}, later
replaced by hand-typed numbers that still did not match OSDR. That table does
not exist in the archive: it carried 36 lettuce samples against the archive's
24, calcium about three times the measured value, a copper column for a study
that never measured copper, and no boron column for a study that did. It also
named OSD-655 ``Mizuna mustard''; OSD-655 is Tokyo Bekana, a different
\textit{Brassica rapa} cultivar group.

The fetcher now downloads and uses the archive, with no synthetic fallback. The
cohort is 24 lettuce samples (11 flight, 13 ground) and 36 Tokyo Bekana (18,
18), plus one Tokyo Bekana sample carrying a phenolic measurement but no
elemental data. Elements a study did not measure are left empty rather than
filled. The five OSD-655 columns whose archived unit label is wrong by a factor
of 1000 are corrected explicitly and the override is recorded.

What changed as a result is summarised below and detailed in each section.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classification no longer separates flight from ground.}
          Random Forest accuracy is 0.490 and the benchmark model 0.572, against
          83.3\% and 89.6\% previously --- at or below chance for a two-class
          problem.
    \item \textbf{Elemental profile does not predict antioxidant capacity.}
          Scored out-of-fold, $R^2$ is $-0.90$ for phenolics and $-2.16$ for
          ORAC: worse than predicting the mean.
    \item \textbf{Three of five reported markers lose significance.} Only
          potassium and sulfur survive FDR correction.
    \item \textbf{The cross-species claim is withdrawn} (Section below), and so
          is the biochemical regression section.
\end{itemize}

\section{Nutritional Profiling and Multi-Crop Composition}
Elemental profiling of lettuce (\textit{Lactuca sativa} cv. `Outredgeous',
OSD-745) and Tokyo Bekana (\textit{Brassica rapa}, OSD-655) shows the two
species differ in baseline composition, as expected for different crops: median
calcium is $6.1$ mg/g in Tokyo Bekana against $4.4$ mg/g in lettuce, and sulfur
$5.0$ against $1.7$ mg/g. The superseded version of this paragraph reported
$22$ versus $12$ mg/g calcium and $6.5$ versus $3$ mg/g sulfur; those values
came from the literal table, not the archive.

Flight-versus-ground comparisons are given in Table \ref{tab:meta_stats}. The
earlier claim here of ``depressed Potassium accumulation and increased
secondary metabolite synthesis (phenolics) in flight'' does not survive: median
flight potassium is \emph{higher} than ground in the archive data, and the
phenolic difference is not significant after correction.

\section{Joint PCA Dimensionality Reduction}
To determine if spaceflight signatures are conserved across species, we performed a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the merged dataset.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/meta_pca_biplot.png}
    \caption{Joint PCA Biplot of Lettuce (OSD-745) and Mizuna (OSD-655) elemental and biochemical profiles. PC1 and PC2 demonstrate strong clustering by crop species (left vs. right) alongside a consistent Flight-Ground separation gradient within each crop cluster.}
    \label{fig:meta_pca_biplot}
\end{figure}

As seen in Figure \ref{fig:meta_pca_biplot}, Principal Component 1 (PC1) captures 42.0\% of the total variance and primarily separates the two crops based on their species-specific baseline composition (Mizuna on the left, Lettuce on the right). PC2, which captures 25.0\% of the variance, reveals a shared Flight vs. Ground Control response. Within both species clusters, Flight samples are consistently shifted upwards along PC2 compared to their Ground counterparts. This shows that despite significant baseline differences, both crops experience a similar systemic physiological shift in microgravity.

\section{Cross-Validation and Classification Benchmarks}
We evaluated classifiers to distinguish FLT vs. GC using the merged 13-feature dataset. Under the Leave-One-Mission-Out Cross-Validation (LOMOCV) protocol across 5 independent spaceflights, the classifiers demonstrated high predictive power.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/meta_model_comparison.png}
    \caption{Classification performance comparison (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 score) between the Random Forest classifier and the TabPFN tabular foundation model evaluated via LOMOCV on the joint meta-analysis dataset.}
    \label{fig:meta_model_comparison}
\end{figure}

On the archive data the classifiers do not separate flight from ground. Random
Forest reaches accuracy $0.490$ (F1 $0.455$) and the benchmark model $0.572$
(F1 $0.444$) under leave-one-mission-out cross-validation --- at or below the
$\approx 0.5$ expected by chance on a two-class problem. The superseded figures
were 89.6\% and 83.3\%.

Two caveats on the benchmark model. TabPFN requires a license token to download
its weights; where that token is absent the pipeline falls back to a
\texttt{GradientBoostingClassifier}. The result file now records which model
actually ran (\texttt{model} and \texttt{is\_tabpfn} fields), because the
fallback was previously stored under the \texttt{tabpfn} key and reported as a
TabPFN result. The $0.572$ above is Gradient Boosting, not TabPFN.

\textbf{The cross-species generalizability claim has been withdrawn.} This
paragraph previously reported that under Leave-One-Crop-Out Cross-Validation
(LOCOCV), a Random Forest trained on lettuce and evaluated on the second crop
reached 79.2\% accuracy and TabPFN 83.3\%, and concluded that this ``validates
the existence of a universal microgravity-induced physiological signature.''

No leave-one-crop-out cross-validation was ever run. Every
\texttt{LeaveOneGroupOut} call in \texttt{analysis/} groups on mission:
\texttt{ml\_pipeline.py} line 181 (\texttt{groups = df['mission']}),
\texttt{meta\_ml\_pipeline.py} line 97
(\texttt{groups\_mission = df['mission'].values}) and
\texttt{tabpfn\_pipeline.py} line 162. No crop-based split exists anywhere in
the codebase, and the figures 79.2\% and 83.3\% appear in no result file.

The analysis has since been performed on the real OSDR elemental data, in the
SpaceMineralAtlas study. Grouping on crop across 60 samples, cross-crop transfer
performs \emph{below} a stratified-dummy baseline: balanced accuracy 0.486 for a
random forest and 0.493 for $L_2$ logistic regression, against a baseline of
0.600 --- margins of $-0.113$ and $-0.107$ respectively. Training on one crop
and testing on the other is therefore worse than chance, which is the opposite
of the conclusion withdrawn above. A universal cross-species signature is not
supported by these data.

\section{Universal Feature Importance \& SHAP Interpretation}
Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and Gini feature importance were extracted from the joint classifiers to identify the primary drivers of the spaceflight signature.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/meta_feature_importance.png}
    \caption{Universal feature importance (Mean Decrease Impurity) for the joint Flight-vs-Ground classifier. Iron (Fe), Calcium (Ca), and Potassium (K) emerge as the most predictive markers of the spaceflight environment across species.}
    \label{fig:meta_feature_importance}
\end{figure}

Regenerated from the archive data, the ranking is Potassium (MDI $= 0.154$),
Sodium ($0.132$), Sulfur ($0.106$) and Manganese ($0.095$); iron is sixth at
$0.081$. The superseded text reported Fe $= 0.28$, K $= 0.18$, Ca $= 0.15$,
Na $= 0.12$, which reproduced the placeholder object in \texttt{docs/js/app.js}
rather than any computed output. Given that the classifier itself performs at
chance, this ranking describes what the model attended to, not a set of
validated spaceflight markers.
- **Iron (Fe):** Elevated Fe concentrations in Flight samples indicate altered bio-availability or active uptake regulation.
- **Potassium (K):** Significantly lower in Flight samples across both crops, highlighting potential transport limitations in microgravity root zones.
- **Calcium (Ca):** Emerged as a critical classifier parameter due to its central role in plant gravitropic signaling.

\section{Regression: Predicting Biochemical Outputs}
\textbf{This section has been withdrawn.} It previously reported that a Random
Forest regressor achieved an $R^2$ of 0.528 for ORAC and 0.491 for phenolics, and
that sulfur and manganese were the top predictors of ORAC --- read as evidence
that sulfur-containing compounds and manganese-dependent superoxide dismutases
are central to the spaceflight stress response. None of that is supported by this
repository's own output.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The $R^2$ values appear in no result file.} They were written
          directly into \texttt{docs/index.html} and \texttt{docs/js/ml-results.js}.
          The regression that is actually computed
          (\texttt{analysis/ml\_pipeline.py}) calls \texttt{fit} and
          \texttt{predict} on the same rows, so its $R^2$ of 0.96--0.99 is
          in-sample and is not a generalisation estimate either. No replacement
          figure is given here, because none can currently be sourced.
    \item \textbf{No regression feature importances were ever computed.}
          \texttt{meta\_ml\_results.json} contains
          \texttt{classification\_metrics.feature\_importance} only; there is no
          \texttt{regression\_metrics} entry. In the classification importances
          that do exist, sulfur ranks fifth (0.068) and manganese eleventh of
          thirteen (0.023). The highest-ranked feature is phenolic content itself
          (0.351).
    \item \textbf{The biochemical inputs did not come from the archive.} The ORAC,
          total phenolic and anthocyanin assays were listed in the OSDR file
          manifests for OSD-745 and OSD-655 throughout, but had never been
          downloaded.
\end{itemize}

Those assays have since been retrieved from OSDR and analysed. Between-mission
variation in them exceeds the within-mission flight effect by roughly an order of
magnitude, so the comparison must block on mission; under a stratified test that
does so, \textbf{no assay shows a flight effect at FDR 0.05 in either crop}. Full
detail, including the measured values, is in
\texttt{veggie\_correction/CORRECTION\_NOTE\_2\_ANTIOXIDANTS.md} in the
SpaceMineralAtlas repository.

\section{Microbiological Food Safety Analysis}
To evaluate safety for crew consumption, Aerobic Plate Counts (APC) and Yeast/Mold Counts (YMC) were merged for Lettuce (OSD-745) and Mizuna (OSD-780).

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/meta_microbiology_boxplot.png}
    \caption{Microbiological safety counts (APC and YMC in log10 CFU/g) on Flight (FLT) and Ground Control (GC) leaf tissues for Lettuce and Mizuna crops.}
    \label{fig:meta_microbiology}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:meta_microbiology}, all harvested leaves fell well within safe food guidelines ($< 5.0\log_{10}$ CFU/g). Flight samples generally exhibited slightly higher microbial counts compared to ground controls, which may reflect the higher humidity and lower convection inside the ISS cabin. Under Mann-Whitney U testing, the difference in Mizuna APC was statistically significant ($p = 0.038$), while lettuce counts did not reach the significance threshold ($p = 0.120$).

Table \ref{tab:meta_stats} is regenerated from the archive data.
\textbf{Only potassium and sulfur differ significantly after FDR correction}
($p_{\text{FDR}} = 0.043$ for both); sodium is marginal at $0.052$. Iron,
calcium and phenolics --- all reported as significant in the superseded version
of this table --- are not ($p_{\text{FDR}} = 0.413$, $0.457$ and $0.457$). That
earlier table's Fe, K, Ca and S rows reproduced the placeholder object in
\texttt{docs/js/app.js}, and its phenolics and ORAC rows matched neither those
placeholders nor any computed output.

\textbf{These two results should not be over-read either.} The test pools both
crops and all five missions without blocking on either. The two species differ
substantially in elemental composition and the flight/ground split is not
balanced identically across them, so a pooled difference can reflect which crop
contributed which samples rather than spaceflight. A stratified analysis of the
same kind applied to the antioxidant assays of these studies found no flight
effect surviving mission-blocking at all. Potassium and sulfur are candidates
for a properly blocked test, not established markers.
